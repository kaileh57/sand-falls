# falling-engine design

Status: draft, 2026-11-05

falling-engine is a custom 2D engine in which every pixel is a simulated cell. It takes the core ideas of Noita's Falling Everything engine and adds three things that engine does not have: bit-exact determinism, first-class multiplayer, and a control surface built for automated agents such as Claude Code to develop and test against. This document covers the architecture, the rules the code must follow, the performance targets, the demos, and the build order.

## Why build a new one

Nolla never released the Falling Everything engine. What they published is Petri Purho's GDC 2019 talk, [Exploring the Tech and Design of Noita](https://gdcvault.com/play/1025695/Exploring-the-Tech-and-Design), and nearly every open source clone copies its design: 64x64 chunks, dirty rects, a checkerboard update order for threads, and rigid bodies traced out of pixels. As of this draft, none of those clones is maintained. [FallingSandEngine](https://github.com/PieKing1215/FallingSandEngine), the most complete, had its last code commit in June 2023. Most stall at the same point, after sand and chunks work and before rigid bodies, performance, and networking are solved together.

Noita itself is not deterministic, which is why the co-op mod [Entangled Worlds](https://github.com/IntQuant/noita_entangled_worlds) has to assign each chunk an owner and send changed pixels over the network. That approach is limited by bandwidth during exactly the moments that make the genre fun, such as a flood or a chain of explosions. falling-engine is designed around determinism from the first commit so that multiplayer sends player inputs instead of pixels.

## Goals and non-goals

The engine targets four outcomes. A large world where millions of cells can be active at once, running at a fixed 60 Hz tick on a desktop CPU. Multiplayer for up to 8 players where bandwidth does not grow with how chaotic the world gets. A headless mode and control API good enough that an agent can write a scenario, run it, inspect the result, and bisect a regression without a human in the loop. And a data-driven material system general enough to build games that look nothing like Noita.

It does not target 3D, mobile in the first version, physically accurate fluid dynamics, or compatibility with Noita content or mods. It is not a general-purpose game engine either. Editors, asset pipelines, and UI frameworks stay minimal until a game needs them.

## Language and dependencies

The engine is written in Rust (stable toolchain, 2024 edition). The multithreaded chunk scheduler is where the hardest bugs live, and Rust's borrow checker rejects most data races at compile time. The tooling also gives the code-quality gates described later without extra infrastructure.

External crates are kept to a short, reviewed list: `winit` for windows and input, `wgpu` for rendering, `rapier2d` for rigid bodies, `rayon` or a small custom work-stealing pool for threads, `serde` for the scene and control formats, and `zstd` for snapshot compression. Godot was considered and dropped. It is MIT licensed, so its code could be reused, but it has nothing for per-cell simulation, and running the sim as a Godot extension would put a binding layer between the engine and every test.

## The determinism contract

Given the same starting world, the same material set, and the same sequence of inputs, every build of the engine on every supported platform produces the same world, bit for bit, regardless of thread count. Multiplayer, replays, regression tests, and agent tooling all rest on this contract, and it cannot be added later.

The contract is enforced by these rules:

1. The cell simulation and particle system use integer or fixed-point math only. Clippy's `float_arithmetic` lint is denied in the simulation crates.
2. There is no shared random number generator in the simulation. Randomness for a cell comes from a hash of (world seed, tick, x, y, purpose), so the value does not depend on which thread runs first.
3. Iteration order is fixed everywhere it can affect results. `HashMap` and `HashSet` with random state are banned in simulation crates through `clippy.toml` `disallowed-types`, and so are wall-clock time and thread-local RNGs.
4. Thread scheduling cannot change results. The checkerboard scheduler (below) is built so that no two chunks updated in parallel write to the same cell.
5. Rigid bodies run in Rapier with its `enhanced-determinism` feature, single-threaded, with bodies inserted and removed in a fixed order. [Rapier's determinism guide](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/rust/determinism/) states that cross-platform results require IEEE 754-2008 compliant hardware and rule out SIMD lane width 8.
6. Rendering, audio, and lighting read from the simulation and never write to it. They are free to use floats and the GPU.

CI checks the contract directly. The same scenario runs with 1 thread and with 32 threads and must produce the same world hash. The same scenario runs on Linux x86_64, Windows x86_64, and macOS ARM64 and must produce the same hash. Any difference fails the build.

## World model

The world is an unbounded grid of cells split into 64x64 chunks. Each cell is 4 bytes: a 12-bit material id, 4 flag bits, an 8-bit temperature in 10-degree steps from -50 C to 2500 C, and an 8-bit material-specific value that holds remaining lifetime, hit points, fall speed for powders, or a packed velocity for liquids. The flags are the tick-parity bit that stops a cell from being processed twice in one tick, the flag that marks a cell as part of a rigid body, and two liquid flags described under liquid motion: the air flag, set on liquid that is falling or flying, and the left flag, the sign of a liquid's speed. Both liquid flags stay clear on every other kind of cell. A chunk is 16 KiB of cell data, small enough to stay in a core's L2 cache while it is updated.

Chunks are stored in a fixed-order map keyed by chunk coordinate. Each chunk tracks a dirty rect, the bounding box of cells that changed last tick, and a chunk whose dirty rect is empty sleeps until a neighbor writes into it. Chunks outside every player's active region are serialized to disk and unloaded, and world generation fills new chunks from the seed so that generation is part of the determinism contract.

Within a chunk, cells update bottom to top. Each row takes two sweeps. The first runs left to right on even ticks and right to left on odd ones and updates every cell except liquid in the air moving the same way; the second runs back the other way and updates only those. Moving liquid is therefore always updated front first, so a jet several cells per tick fast does not run into its own tail, and the other cells alternate direction so that nothing drifts to one side.

Liquids move in two ways, described in their own section below. Resting liquid moves as columns of shallow water in a flow phase that runs once a tick after the cell passes, and liquid in the air moves as single cells with a velocity. Together they collapse, surge, run up slopes, slosh, pour and fall like a real liquid, and they come to rest level and sleep.

Powder cells fall with a speed stored in their 8-bit value, in sixteenths of a cell per tick, capped at 255 (about 16 cells per tick). Gravity adds 7 speed units every 16 ticks, spread over the ticks by a fixed dither on the tick number rather than by randomness. A cell moving slower than one cell per tick hovers in place and keeps its speed until it has built up a whole step. A falling cell blocked by another falling cell takes the slower of the two speeds instead of stopping, so a stream stays together instead of breaking into spray. A powder cell sinking through a lighter liquid swaps with it on half of its tries; when a try fails it keeps its speed (up to 1.5 cells per tick, its sinking speed) and tries again next tick instead of spreading out over the surface. Gases and fire, which have lifetimes in their value, move one cell per tick. Gas caught under a liquid bubbles up through it whatever its density, so blast dust in a lake washes out.

Heat moves between neighboring cells. Each material has a heat capacity (by default 20 for static solids, 14 for powders, 30 for liquids and 1 for gases and fire), and an exchange changes each side in inverse proportion to its capacity: with a rate k from the two conductivities, the cell gains diff * k * c_other / (128 * (c_self + c_other)) and its neighbor loses the matching share, each rounded stochastically from separate random bits. The total of capacity times temperature is conserved in expectation, and with equal capacities the rule reduces to the old one exactly. Air holds 1/50 of what a metal cell does, so it conducts freely without draining hot metal: it warms up, rises and shows as heat haze.

Melting, boiling and freezing can have a latent heat. A cell past its threshold changes with a chance of overshoot / latent per tick and is otherwise held at the threshold, so it sits on a plateau and takes in or gives off about the latent heat first. Water boils with 540 C of latent heat, so a quench boils little water, and molten metal and lava freeze with 300 C and 250 C, so a pour stays liquid long enough to fill a mold. Ice and snow take in 160 C before they melt and water gives off 80 C before it freezes, so ice floes in a sea at 0 C last a whole scene instead of melting in seconds. A liquid that freezes while it is falling, hanging over open space, or resting on a different fluid or powder becomes its solid's debris (lava turns to rubble, molten metal to scrap) instead of a floating static crust. A crust on its own melt stays static if that melt stands on something solid or is at least 8 cells deep. On a few cells of melt that are falling or lie on rubble, the melt freezes to debris or runs off in turn, and the crust was left hanging in the air: a lava drop could leave a cell of basalt floating four rows above its heap. Steam holds six times the heat of air and gives off 150 C of latent heat before it condenses back to water, so a quench or a lava front sends up a plume that lasts a few seconds before it rains back. Emitters such as sprinklers and lava sources push through gas, so they keep going in the steam or smoke they make.

Reaction products start at the reactants' capacity-weighted mean temperature, or at the reaction's `temp` if that is hotter. Water on lava gives hot steam and glowing obsidian, water on burning wood leaves wood below its ignition point, which is how water puts fires out, and water on an ember or a flame flashes into steam at 150 C that rises instead of condensing on the spot. A liquid that reacts into a solid follows the freezing rule: the obsidian skin where water meets lava crumbles to gravel and sinks unless it rests on lava or rock, so the contact keeps renewing and a lava front entering a lake keeps boiling it.

## Liquid motion

A naive falling-sand liquid moves one cell at a time and only sideways into air, so a column of water drains a few cells per tick, a deep reservoir flows no faster than a puddle, and a collapsing face turns into a spray of single cells. falling-engine moves a body of liquid as a whole instead. Liquid at rest on something is a set of columns of shallow water, and a flow phase steps them with the shallow water equations once a tick. Liquid in the air (a falling stream, a jet, a sheet poured over an edge, a splash) moves as single cells along a straight line until it lands on a column and joins it. A 100-row column of water collapses as the shallow water equations say it should, its surface sloping smoothly down from the wall to a front running at about 2.4 cells per tick (2 sqrt(g h) is 3.3 for a frictionless bed), and a surge carries its momentum up a slope and slides back. Holes, pipes, taps and gates below the surface are part of the flow: pressure drives liquid through them at about the rate Torricelli's law gives, however thick the wall and however the pipe turns.

### Two codes in one byte

A liquid cell stores one of two codes in its 8-bit value, told apart by the air flag. Resting liquid stores a flow speed: the value is m and the speed is m squared in 1/4096 cells per tick, up to 15.9 cells per tick, pointing left when the left flag is set. Only the top cell of a column carries the speed; the cells under it have value 0 and both flags clear, which is exactly what a new cell is, so scenes and snapshots need no migration. Liquid in the air stores a 4-bit horizontal speed code (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 40, 48, 64, 80, 112 and 160 sixteenths of a cell per tick, signed by the left flag) and a 4-bit fall speed code (0, 2, 4, 7, 11, 16, 22, 30, 40, 52, 67, 85, 108, 140, 190 and 255). A speed between two codes is stored as one of them at random, with the chance that keeps it right on average; liquid flying sideways rounds its fall speed by the places it passes instead (see below). Liquids whose value holds a lifetime keep no speed between ticks.

### Order within a tick

A tick runs the four checkerboard passes, then a fall sweep, then the flow phase, then the pressure pass. The checkerboard passes update every cell except liquid that is in the air or has nothing under it; for resting liquid they run heat and reactions, offer each column's top cell to the flow phase, and let a pressed cell beside a hole in a wall jet out of it. A job that met liquid in the air also runs in the fall sweep, which goes over chunk rows from the bottom of the world up and splits each row into two halves by chunk x parity, so jobs that run at the same time stay two chunks apart. Liquid falling across a chunk border therefore always finds the liquid below it already moved, and a falling mass keeps its shape across borders. Sideways that is true only within a row's half: liquid flying across a border into a chunk of the half that runs later meets the liquid ahead of it there not yet moved. Such a cell waits, and so does liquid behind or above a cell that waited; the chunks where any liquid waited run again after both halves, in the same order, by which time the liquid in the way has moved. Which half runs first flips every tick. With one round and the even half always first, a dense jet or sheet piled up against every other border, then came out past it thinned into a row of bars. A resting cell whose support starts to fall during the fall sweep moves in the same sweep, so a mass with nothing under it falls as one piece. Where liquid in a chunk's top row is in the air, the columns above it in the chunk above join the sweep on the same tick, even if that chunk was asleep or no liquid in it was in the air when the passes ran, so the fall reaches the top of a block in one tick instead of one chunk row per tick, and a block does not tear into slabs at chunk borders with its upper part asleep in the air.

### Liquid in the air

A cell in the air gains gravity each tick and moves along a straight line toward where its velocity takes it, swapping with the air, gas or lighter fluid it passes through. A cell falling straight down rounds its speeds with random bits drawn once per tick, not per cell, and steps on a dither phase shared by every cell, so cells moving at one speed move together and a falling blob stays a blob. A cell that comes down on falling liquid or powder rides it instead of landing on it, and gains gravity like the cell under it, so a falling column speeds up as one; on slower liquid of its kind in the air the two share their fall speed, as viscosity would make them. Cells do not chase the liquid below a gap: a cell falling straight down used to fall a little faster than falling liquid up to six cells below it until the gap closed, which turned a stream fed a cell at a time into rods that broke away one after another, a dashed line. Each cell of a stream now falls from where it started, so the stream's cells are spaced as far apart as they move in a tick, the way a stream thins as it speeds up.

A falling mass gains gravity by the tick, so it falls as one; by the place, the rows of a falling slab would cross each place one after another and pull apart. A stream is the other way round: its cells leave one after another, and each should speed up at the same places as the cells before it. Liquid falling straight down out of a hole in a floor (see the flow phase), and a lone cell dropping from rest with none of its kind beside it or on top of it, a drip off a tap or a ledge, carry a stream mark: no sideways speed and the left flag, which such liquid does not otherwise use. A drip leaves at the speed of a cell that has fallen one row. Marked liquid gains its speed by the rows it crosses, with the dither by place that liquid flying sideways uses (below), so every cell of the stream has one speed at each row and the stream thins smoothly as it falls. Gaining by the tick with bits shared by every cell, cells of every age in a drain moved up a code together and pulled away from the cells that had just left, so the drain of a pool through a hole a cell wide fell as rods about 30 cells long with gaps of 60 between them, and drips from a tap met at different speeds, rode on one another and fell as clumps with gaps of 40 to 60 rows.

Liquid flying sideways (a jet, a sheet poured over an edge) needs more than that, because its cells leave one after another and should all follow one path: the stream is steady while each cell in it speeds up as it falls. With bits drawn per tick, cells of every age in a jet shared one fall speed, and whenever the shared speed went up a train of cells broke off, so a jet fell as a staircase of dashes and then as separate bars. Such a cell steps where the line of its path through whole cells does. While it flies a cell a tick sideways or faster, it takes the tick's dither of columns like every moving cell, and at each column it crosses it steps down the rows the line gives there, however steep its path; slower than that and falling faster than it flies, it takes the tick's dither of rows and steps aside where the line does. Its fall speed goes up by places too: at each column it crosses (or row, on a slow steep path) it moves up to the next code where a dither fires whose rate is half the share of the gap between codes that gravity covers in that step. From wherever a cell enters a code it then keeps it as long as gravity takes to cross the code's share of speeds on average, and its code is its speed rounded to the nearest code. The steps and the gains are worked out one place at a time with the speed the cell has there, so the cells of a stream take one path through the same cells however the ticks split their moves, and a cell held up on its way waits on its path with the gains of the places it crossed. Worked out over a tick's whole move with the speed the cell started the tick with, and with no gain for a cell held up, two cells that crossed one column on different ticks came out a row or a code apart, and the steep part of a jet through a wall 6 cells thick broke into two or three parallel trails of dashes about 10 cells apart. Keyed by rows wherever the path was steep, each cell of a slice poured into one column on one tick stepped aside at its own rows, the slice sheared across the path of the slice ahead, and a sheet off a ledge jammed into a block of liquid standing in the air. Every cell of a jet poured with one velocity then takes one path, a smooth parabola: a jet from a hole 52 rows under the surface arcs out and lands within about 15% of where ballistics puts it. A cell moving sideways goes on moving sideways while it rides. Liquid in the air ahead of it holds it up for the tick: liquid moving its way but slower takes on half the difference in speed and the cell keeps the rest, so a stream evens out its speed instead of piling up, and behind liquid that does not move its way it keeps its speed and goes on once that has fallen out of the way. Held up by anything else, it falls straight down for the rest of the tick as far as its fall speed takes it, so two cells flying at each other do not hold each other up in the air for good. When the cell behind took the slower speed instead, a lane of a jet that met one slow cell went on at its speed for good and fell away as a second trail, and a cell that had stopped stopped every cell behind it, so a sheet over a weir jammed at the lip and fell as a comb of bars. Cells side by side across a path do not share their speed: sharing pulled single cells off the edge of a stream onto paths of their own, drawn as a second thin trail beside the foot of a jet. Liquid that falls through an opening in the floor of a body that stands on a floor, one too wide to hold it (see holes, pipes and gates), leaves at the Torricelli speed for its head, sqrt(2 g h); only a floor that cannot move this tick counts, because liquid beside the opening that stands on more liquid may be about to fall itself. A one-cell void under a resting column is closed by moving the column's top cell down into it.

On landing, a cell's fall turns into sideways speed on a solid or a rigid body, keeping (256 - viscosity)^2 / 256 of the impact energy, and is absorbed on liquid or powder. A cell that lands on its own liquid becomes the top of that column and shares its sideways speed with the column, weighted by up to 24 cells of depth. One hard landing in eight of a liquid thinner than viscosity 100 throws the cell back up as a free particle, at most 24 per job per tick.

### The flow phase

The flow phase builds bodies from the column tops the passes offered, in sorted order. A column is a run of resting liquid of one material and the floor under it. From a seed column the phase walks left and right, linking each neighbor whose surface or floor the column's liquid can reach: a neighbor of the same liquid that sees the column back, a dry neighbor whose floor is at most two rows lower or six rows higher, up to 32 dry columns past the last wet one. A neighbor farther below is a brink. Liquid of the body's kind hanging over open space in the way, about to fall, is looked past: seen as a wall, a few cells poured past a lip that had landed on falling liquid closed the brink for a tick, and the flow piled up against the lip into a spike. A wet column with a wall beside its surface looks down that side for a hole: the liquid in the hole, or the floor past it, is its neighbor there. So a tank links to the hole in its wall, the hole to the next column of the hole, and the last to whatever lies outside, through a wall of any thickness and around corners, since a column that turns up or down a pipe finds the next one the same way. A full pipe that ends over a shaft no wider than eight cells, under the pipe's roof, goes on down the shaft, which fills, rather than pouring into it. Every column belongs to one body per tick.

A body that is not at rest is stepped with a finite volume scheme: HLL fluxes with hydrostatic reconstruction over the bed, in 16.16 fixed point, with as many substeps as the fastest wave needs at a Courant number of 3/4, at most 48. Edges where liquid is pressed through an opening under a roof are pipes, stepped as described below. Each column loses speed to bed friction, to a viscous drag that grows as the layer thins, and to a small steady loss. A brink edge lets liquid out at the speed of a wave running onto a dry bed. Where the bed steps, the deeper column's flow crosses the edge only in the part of it that reaches over the step, so that part carries the column's whole discharge, at up to the speed of a wave over the step. Flow onto or off a cliff deep under the surface then passes what reaches the cliff, and the surface dips over the cliff by the few rows that speeding up takes, as Bernoulli's law says. With the column's own speed at the edge instead, the step passed less than reached it, and the surface stood a step high above the cliff.

Whole cells show a level surface as bumps of a row. An edge between columns that are level within a row is flat when a line fitted by least squares to the surface over 24 columns on either side (as many as the run has, next to a wall) falls less than a row over 96 columns. A flat edge carries the flow that already moves through it but no push from the one-row step, so a level body does not churn, while a gentle lean that only shows over the window still drives the flow. An earlier rule called every edge within eight columns of a wall flat, and so held back the water by the wall of a collapsing column: it kept a plateau that ended in a cliff, 30 rows too high at the wall at tick 60. The depths the column now has at tick 40 and 60 are within three rows of a fine-grid solution of the shallow water equations.

The shallow water equations turn a bore, the step where fast shallow flow runs into slower deep water, into a jump one column wide. In cells that is a wall of liquid hundreds of rows tall standing in a dam break. A real bore's front is a breaking roller as long as the water is deep, so where columns close on each other the solver mixes momentum between them with an eddy viscosity of (depth / 4)^2 times the closing speed, the mixing length being a quarter of the depth. The closing speed is the fastest within two edges, so the roller covers the toe of the bore, and the roller acts only where the closing speed over a mixing length is at least an eighth of the wave speed, so gentle sloshing is untouched. Nor does it act across a step in the bed taller than its mixing length: the flow speeds up over such a step, and mixing it with the slower deep flow behind held it back until liquid piled up into a spike over the cliff. The mixing is implicit, one tridiagonal solve per run of coupled columns per substep, so it is stable however strong it gets, and it moves no liquid: a bore keeps its height and speed and only its front slopes.

The cells then follow the columns. The liquid moved through each edge over the tick is rounded to whole cells at random with the right mean and limited to what each column holds. Liquid that leaves into the air over a brink or a step is rounded along an even sequence instead, the tick times the golden ratio, so a trickle leaves at a steady rhythm and falls as a line; rounded at random, it left in bursts with gaps of up to ten ticks between them and fell as dashes. Rightward transfers are carried out left to right and leftward ones right to left, each as a queue of top cells taken from columns that lose liquid and set on top of columns that gain it, every move a swap with the air or lighter fluid it replaces. Cells that a column has no room for go back to the columns they came from. Passed on, as they were, they were stacked on the next column with room however high: a column under a floating crate that had one free cell took in what the solver sent it, and the rest stood beside the crate as a tower of water up to 11 cells high every few dozen ticks while the crate bobbed. Where flow runs into a column that liquid of its kind is falling onto, the falling cell it meets lands on the column in place of the cell flowing in, and the cell it stood in for flows on, so a falling curtain lands as fast as the flow pushes into it instead of standing as a wall that the flow piles up against. Sent back to its column instead, it held up the foot of a waterfall, and the sheet off a weir broke into strands. Liquid that leaves over a brink is poured as cells in the air into the block of cells it crosses in a tick: as many columns past the lip as it moves per tick, and no higher than the column is deep, filled from the lip up and outward. It leaves at the speed that carries what the edge passed over the tick through the column's depth, so the sheet's top is level with the column's surface, rounded to a whole number of cells per tick, at least one, so the sheet moves the same number of columns on every tick and each tick's slice of it is as tall as the last. Blocks poured on successive ticks lie side by side and leave as one sheet that arcs down. A cell poured into the first column past the lip leaves with a slow fall; further out it has the fall speed and the drop that the path from the first column gives there, so it is where liquid that left the lip earlier in the tick has got to, and every cell of the stream follows one path. Poured further out with the speed of the first column, it skipped the places where the path steps down a row or gains speed, and the lanes of a jet set out a row or a code apart. Poured one column per tick, up to 24 cells high, the flood off a block in a dam break was a barcode of single-cell lines two or three columns apart that hung level with the block's top, and at most 24 cells a tick got past, so the lee of the block stayed dry. Poured at the column's speed plus the speed of a shallow wave, the block was wider than the outflow and filled only to half its height, so the column stood above the sheet as a face; and at a speed between whole cells the sheet moved one column on one tick and two on the next, so the outflow filled one column to the top and then two to half their height, and the sheet wore a comb of bars. Where liquid or a floor lies fewer than 8 rows below the lip, the poured cells first fill the space down there, at rest: the sheet hugs the face and lands at once instead of shooting out over a thin lens of air that read as a crack in the water. The end of a pipe that opens over a drop pours a jet instead, into the rows of its opening, at the speed of the flow through the opening contracted to 0.62 of it (the vena contracta), which with the pipe's head loss is the Torricelli speed sqrt(2 g h). At the speed of the flow through the whole opening, 0.6 of that, a jet landed half as far out as ballistics says. A jet takes the speed code nearest its speed. Both keep the speed of the liquid poured just before them while their own is close to it (within a code for a jet, three quarters of a cell per tick for a sheet), so cells poured on different ticks fly along one path: a speed that hovered between two codes was poured at one on one tick and the other on the next, and the faster slices caught up with the slower ones ahead and tore the sheet into bars. Nothing is ever poured over a wall above a hole.

A step in the bed is a fall when the liquid crossing it drops far: the floor past the step lies more than two rows lower, and the surface there more than two rows and more than a third of the upper column's depth lower, as over a weir whose tail water reaches less than two thirds of the way up the head. The two columns stay one body, but in the solver the upper column runs out over the edge as over a brink, and the lower one takes the liquid in with none of its momentum and sees a wall; the cells that cross are poured into the air past the lip like a sheet off a brink, and bring their momentum when they land. What finds no room in the air goes on onto the lower column's top. The lower column takes the liquid in only as its cells land: counted in it at once, the liquid was passed on downstream before it arrived, and the column at the foot of the step sank into a well a cell wide that showed as a slit of air between the step's face and the water past it. Where the tail water lies within 8 rows of the lip or over it, the falling liquid first fills the column at the foot of the step, at rest, up to the level of the tail water beyond it, and only the rest is poured into the air: it plunges into the tail water rather than falling through air. Poured as a sheet that flew on past that column, it left the column fed by nothing, and the column sank into a notch a few cells wide or a slit one cell wide. As one body with an ordinary edge at the step, the columns passed on what they took in and no cell moved: in a dam break the flood on top of a stone block stood 28 rows above the block's lee with a still, vertical face for 140 ticks while the solver passed 38 cells a tick over it, and once the lee drained the flood poured off as a sheet that stood bridged over an air pocket with a comb of bars hanging from it. A step deep under the surface, where the surfaces on either side differ by a ripple, is no fall. The new flow speed goes on each column's new top cell. Slow flow between two flat edges stops, and so does flow slower than a quarter of a cell per tick in a film one or two cells deep, which is what lets films on a slope stick and drain.

### Holes, pipes and gates

A hole in a wall below the surface, a pipe, a tap or a sluice gate is a run of edges where liquid is pressed through an opening under a roof: an edge beside a column that is full and cannot rise, or an edge where the surface on one side stands above the roof of the opening. Each edge's opening runs from the higher of the two floors to the lower of the two roofs, a roof being the first thing above a column that liquid cannot pass, looked for up to 64 rows. A run of such edges joined through full columns is one pipe, and the liquid in it moves as one slug, since a full column can neither gain nor lose liquid: the same flow q passes every edge of the pipe. The two ends of a pipe are the columns that are not full, or a wall, which stops it, or a brink, which takes only outflow. Each substep, q changes by g times the difference in head between the ends, divided by the pipe's inertance (the sum of 1 / height over its full columns and one opening), less the head loss (1 + K) q |q| / (2 a^2), with a the narrowest opening and 1 + K = 2.6 plus a tenth for every cell of length per cell of height, and less the drag a column as deep as the pipe is high would feel. The losses are taken implicitly, so the step is stable however short the pipe. The head at an end is the column's surface, or the middle of the opening if the surface is lower, since liquid leaving into the air there is under no pressure. In a steady state q is Torricelli's sqrt(2 g h) times the opening times a discharge coefficient of about 0.6: a tank 100 rows deep drains through a 4-cell hole at its floor at 5.3 cells per tick through a 4-cell wall, 5.1 through a 10-cell wall and 5.0 through a 16-cell wall, against about 2, 1 and 1 when liquid left a hole only as single jetting cells, and at 5.8 through a culvert that turns down a shaft and out under a 16-cell wall. The column a pipe flows into takes the momentum of the jet, q times q over the opening, and both end columns otherwise see a wall. The flow is stored on the top cells of the full columns, as each column's speed, so it carries over from tick to tick and connected vessels swing past level and back before they settle. Every edge of a pipe rounds the liquid it moved with the same random bits, so its full columns pass on exactly what they take in.

A pipe holds only while it is pressed: while the surface at one of its ends stands above the roof of the opening there. Once both ends are lower and one of them is open to the air (a brink, or a column with nothing over it within 64 rows), air reaches the full columns and their liquid runs out as open channel flow; for that tick they are ordinary columns that can lose liquid. A pipe closed by a wall, or whose ends lie under a roof themselves, as with liquid shut in under a floating crate, stays a pipe. Without this rule a hole drained a tank only while its surface stood above the hole's middle: the full columns in the hole could pass liquid on but never lose it, so a 4-cell hole at the floor stayed full, standing above the tank's surface on one side and a film on the other, the tank kept 365 cells after 10,000 ticks and never slept, and a tank with a hole halfway up drained at 0.01 cells per tick once its surface came within two rows of the hole's middle. Now the 100-row tank empties to a film one row deep and sleeps by tick 5,500 (5,750 through a 12-cell wall), and the tank with the hole halfway up drains to the hole's floor, every cell kept, and sleeps by tick 4,000.

A hole in a floor holds the liquid over it. Resting liquid with open space or falling liquid under it, liquid of its kind on top of it, and a solid edge (a static cell or resting powder, never a body) within 8 cells on either side along the row under it, or along its own row where liquid of its kind hangs between the edges, does not fall. The flow phase drains such a column through the hole as through an orifice, at the Torricelli speed sqrt(2 g h) for its depth contracted to 0.62 of the hole, and what drains takes its share of the column's momentum. The drained liquid is rounded to whole cells along the golden ratio sequence and poured below the hole as a stream, into the cells it crosses in a tick from the hole down, each with the fall speed its path from the hole gives there (see liquid in the air). Left to fall, the column over a hole fell as one rod as deep as the pool, the pool refilled it, and a pool's drain through a hole a cell wide ran as rods 30 cells long with gaps of 60 between them. A body is no such floor: it moves, and with its cells as edges water held between a mast and a deck edge, or on a floating spar, piled 20 to 100 rows over the sea and never levelled. Liquid under a roof within 24 rows, a full pipe that turns down a shaft, is pressed down the shaft instead, and liquid over a void inside a body, with resting liquid of its kind at most 8 rows under the gap, falls in and closes it.

### Rest and settle moves

A body is at rest when every column is slower than 0.08 cells per tick, no step between neighbors is taller than two rows, plus a row for every 256 rows of depth, and no pipe joins two ends whose heads differ by more than two rows. In deep liquid a step of a few rows is a ripple that whole cells can only show to within a row or two, and rounding the solver's flows to cells would only stir it again, so a large body would never be still all at once. A body at rest is not stepped. Its speeds are cleared, and settle moves level what is left: each still column looks along the surface for a column at least two rows lower, over columns lower than itself, up to 2,048 columns away, and moves its top cell there, or pours it over a brink at the end of such a surface. A body makes at most 64 settle moves per tick plus one for every eight columns. Every move lowers a cell, so the body comes to rest level within a row, nothing changes any more, and its chunks sleep. A moving body makes the same moves for still films on slopes, which its flow leaves behind, but pours a film over a brink only from within two columns of it: from further in, the front of a flow running out over a film onto a ledge had its top cell leap over the film every tick and never got deep enough to move on. A cell poured over a brink by a settle move goes into the rows the end column fills, not up to 24 rows above the lip, where a film running off a floating crate stacked into a column of cells hanging in the air. It leaves at a cell a tick sideways, as the slowest sheet does, so a film trickling off a ledge falls along the path of the stream poured over the ledge before it; at half that speed it fell along a path of its own, and the stream off the ledge was two trails of drops.

### Column head tables

The flow phase and the jets need the extent of a run of resting liquid that can be thousands of cells, far past the 32-cell reach. Each chunk keeps derived tables: for each column, the run of resting liquid going up from its bottom row and going down from its top row, the head coming in from the chunks above, and the run continuing into the chunks below. Before the passes, the world rebuilds the run tables for every chunk with a dirty rect or replaced cells, then sums the head down each column and the run below up each column in a fixed chunk order. A scan for a column's top or floor walks its own chunk and then jumps by the tables. The tables depend only on the cells at the start of the tick, because every rule write that can change them marks the cell dirty, so they need no hashing or snapshotting; loading cells marks the tables stale. Liquid in the air does not count: a free-falling stream presses on nothing.

### Pressure pass

Two vessels joined below their surfaces in a way a row of columns cannot follow, such as a pool joined to another through a hole in its floor, are not one body, so the flow phase cannot level them. The pressure pass takes resting surface cells on top of liquid and searches the resting liquid of the same kind under each, breadth first in a fixed order with a bitmap for visited cells, for an opening at least two rows lower that it would rest in, up to 4,096 cells, and moves the cell there. The search does not pass through liquid in the air, so a stream falling from a tap into a mold does not join the two into one vessel. It sorts and deduplicates its candidates and handles a window of 16 of them, starting at an offset that turns with the tick so every candidate gets its turn. A candidate that finds nothing gets the settled mark, a resting cell with no speed and the left flag set, and does not ask again until something moves it; candidates outside the window are marked dirty so they ask again next tick. Every move lowers a cell. The pass runs after the flow phase and leaves alone liquid in the bodies the flow phase stepped on the tick: in a body that is flowing, it found the surface lower further along and moved surface cells there, which piled them up into a hump in a collapsing column.

### Viscosity

Every liquid uses the same rules, and the viscosity in its material definition sets the rest. A flowing column loses 120 + 4 v in 1/65,536 of its speed for every cell it flows over a cell-deep bed (less on a deeper one, in proportion), v^3 / 64 divided by its depth squared in 1/65,536 of its speed per tick to viscous drag, and a steady 24 + (v - 20)^2 / 4 in 1/65,536 of its speed per tick. Water (20) has almost no drag; lava (160) loses 7.5% of its speed every tick and empties half of a 100-row column in 141 ticks against 64 for water, then stops in a heap. Impacts on solids keep (256 - v)^2 / 256 of their energy, and only liquids thinner than 100 splash. In order of viscosity, water, acid, oil, molten metal and lava collapse later and spread less far.

### Determinism and cost

The flow phase runs on one thread after the parallel passes and depends only on the cells: the top cell of each column carries its speed, and nothing else is kept between ticks. Bodies are stepped in sorted seed order, all arithmetic is integer, and random rounding uses the position-keyed hash, so results do not depend on the thread count. Cells it writes are marked dirty in one go at the end of the phase, one rectangle per chunk, which gives the same dirty rects as marking the 3x3 neighborhood of each write. In the dam break benchmark (4 million water cells released at once) a tick takes 10 to 15 ms on 4 threads in the build container, depending on the load of the shared machine, against 15 to 16 ms in the same runs for the flow phase before pipes, and 18 to 34 ms measured earlier for the one-cell-at-a-time rule that came before momentum liquids, with 154,000 cells visited per tick instead of 900,000. About 4 ms of the tick is the flow phase; finding pipes adds a scan only where neighboring surfaces differ. A block of water hanging in the air costs more than it did, because all of it falls at once: every one of the 153,600 cells of the `water_falling_512x300` block is checked in the main passes and moved in the fall sweep on every tick. Before the fall sweep took in the chunks above a fall, the upper two thirds of that block fell asleep in the air and the benchmark visited 56,000 cells per tick; it now takes 1.7 to 1.9 times as long on one thread and 1.5 to 2 times as long on four as it did then (83 to 90 ms against 44 to 49 ms for ten ticks on one thread, under load), though each visited cell costs about a fifth fewer instructions. The fall sweep's second round runs only for chunks where liquid waited, and away from chunk borders the check for waiting is a comparison, so a block falling straight down costs what it did before (75 to 77 ms for ten ticks on one thread and 44 to 45 ms on four, against 76 to 77 and 46 to 47 in the same runs); a first version that read the cell below every falling cell made it a fifth slower on one thread.

## World scale

Every system uses one scale: 10 cells per meter, and 60 ticks per second. Gravity is one constant in `fe-core`, `GRAVITY = Fx(1792)`, which is 7/256 cells per tick squared, 98.4 cells per second squared, or 9.84 m/s^2. Cells, free particles, rigid bodies and the avatar all fall with it; the rigid body world converts it to Rapier units with `CELLS_PER_METER`, and a unit test checks that the two agree. A water cell weighs 1 kg. Scenes are built to the same scale: a 4 m floor is 40 cells, and the 12-cell avatar is a small 1.2 m figure. Before this, cells, particles and bodies fell at 450 cells per second squared and the avatar at 900, so every fall played more than twice as fast as it should and a collapsing building looked like a time lapse.

## Parallel scheduling

The scheduler follows the checkerboard scheme from the Noita talk. Chunks are split into four groups by the parity of their x and y chunk coordinates, and each tick runs four passes, one per group. Chunks in the same group are two chunks apart, so if every update writes at most 32 cells past its own chunk border, two chunks in the same pass can never touch the same cell. That limit means a cell moves at most 32 cells per tick (1,920 cells per second at 60 Hz). Anything faster is a free particle, described below.

The order of the passes flips every tick across vertical chunk borders and every two ticks across horizontal ones. A flow that crosses a border is then updated front first on half of the ticks whichever way it runs. With a fixed rotation, some borders went back first three ticks out of four for flows to the right, and the face of a collapsing column stuck at a chunk border for a hundred ticks.

Two chunks in the same pass can both write into the gap chunk between them, so dirty-rect updates to neighbors go through atomic min/max operations. The merged result is the same in any order, which keeps it deterministic.

The scheduler needs mutable access to a chunk and parts of its eight neighbors at once, which safe Rust cannot express. This is the only place in the engine where `unsafe` is allowed. It lives in one module, every block has a `// SAFETY:` comment that states the invariant it relies on, and the module's tests run under Miri in CI.

## Materials and reactions

Materials are data, loaded from files in the scene or game directory. A material definition includes its state (static, powder, liquid, gas, or fire), density, how far it spreads per tick, flammability, how heat moves through it, its melting and boiling behavior, and its render palette. Reactions are rules of the form A touching B becomes C and D, with a probability given as an integer out of 65,536 so that it stays inside the integer-only rule.

At load time the material set is compiled into lookup tables indexed by material id, so the inner loop never branches on strings or walks a list. The compiled set has a hash, and a multiplayer client cannot join a server whose material hash differs from its own.

Cells with a lifetime (fire, smoke, embers) count it down each tick with a chance set by `lifetime_rate`, so an 8-bit lifetime can last longer than 255 ticks. When it runs out, the cell becomes its `residue` with the residue's chance and its `decay` otherwise. Burning wood, leaves and coal decay mostly to smoke and leave ash or embers only sometimes, so a burned forest leaves a thin layer of ash instead of one ash cell per plank. Burning wood leaves charcoal a third of the time: it smoulders dim red for about fifteen seconds, giving off smoke, so a burned forest ends in smoking black stumps, and water quenches it to coal. Smoke lives about ten seconds and thins out over its last two. Embers are heavy sparks: they glow at 400 C for a second or so, fall and drift downwind, and water puts them out with a puff of steam.

Oil that catches fire becomes burning oil, a liquid as light as oil that floats on water, throws flames up and heats the oil beside it, and burns for two to four seconds before it turns to smoke, so a slick on the sea burns across instead of flashing once and going out. A torch under water goes out and is left a wooden stick.

Gases rise or sink by buoyancy. Each tick a gas tries a vertical move first with a chance of |density| * 32 / 256, up when it is lighter than air and down when it is heavier, and drifts sideways otherwise. Steam always rises, smoke rises on 62% of ticks and mist on 12%, so mist lingers in a cave while steam boils off. Dust is a gas slightly heavier than air that hangs and settles for about eight seconds; explosions and shattering bodies release it, always into air cells, so no mass is lost. Fire in wind tilts downwind with a chance of |wind| / 4 and keeps some sideways jitter even in the strongest wind.

The engine knows nothing about wands, spells, or any other game concept. Everything game-specific sits above the material layer.

## Free particles

Cells that are thrown, splashed, or blasted leave the grid and become free particles with fixed-point position and velocity. Particles are stored per chunk, updated in spawn-id order, and turned back into cells when they land. They fall with the same gravity as everything else. A particle that lands where no neighboring cell is free searches further out, then bounces up for a while, and finally takes the first free cell up its column, so particles do not delete what they land in. This is how the engine handles movement faster than 32 cells per tick, and it keeps explosions from breaking the checkerboard rule.

A particle carries its whole cell, temperature included, and lives by the grid's cell rules while it flies. Each tick it trades heat with the air, gas or fire it is passing through, its material's cooling and held temperature apply, its lifetime counts down, and it melts, freezes or catches fire at the same thresholds as a cell. A molten metal speck thrown from a blast cools from about 1,900 C, sits on its freezing plateau for a moment, and lands as cold scrap; an ember burns out in the air; a splinter hot enough to ignite lands burning. A particle that becomes a gas leaves the flight as that gas. Burning particles lose a sixteenth of their speed to the air each tick, so sparks fly out, stall and drift down. Explosives do not go off in flight; they go off where they land.

### Explosions

An explosion damages solid cells in its radius, heats the air in it, sets the air near its center on fire and throws debris, powders and liquids out as particles. Blasts with a radius of 12 or more also flash: the inner third fills with `flash`, a fire at 1,500 C that lasts a few ticks and lights everything around, and throws burning sparks. Debris with ground under it in the blast bounces up out of the crater; debris with open space below it flies straight out from the center, so a shell bursting in the air throws its stars in a ring. A blast heats what it hits by at most 800 C, so a crater wall glows but repeated blasts in one place do not melt rock.

Explosive cells that a blast reaches are not destroyed but set off: they are heated past their trigger and go off on the next tick. All explosions in one tick are merged before they run. Each explosive cell adds to the blast it overlaps, whose center moves to the mean of its cells and whose radius and power grow with each one, up to a radius of 48, and blasts that grow into each other merge too. So a stack of 144 TNT cells goes up as one large blast at its middle with a flash that lights the room, and a charge that a blast reaches goes off with it, instead of a crackle of small overlapping pops.

## Rigid and destructible bodies

A rigid body is a connected group of cells that moves as one piece, such as a falling slab of rock, a wooden crate, or a ship. Bodies come from three places: authored shapes placed by a scene or a game (crates, vehicles, doors), terrain that loses its support, and debris from a body that breaks apart. All three end up as the same thing in the engine, so every body can be damaged and split.

### Building a body

A flood fill finds the connected cells, and the cells are merged into rectangles (horizontal runs joined with identical runs below) that become a compound of Rapier cuboids. Terrain near bodies gets the same treatment: one compound of cuboids per chunk, built from its static cells. An earlier version used one Rapier voxel collider per chunk, but Rapier often generated no contact between a voxel shape and a body, and bodies sank into flat ground. While a body moves, its cells are removed from the grid. At the end of each tick they are stamped back at the body's new position so that sand, water, and other bodies collide with them.

### Structural support

Solid materials are either anchored (bedrock, and anything a scene marks as fixed) or structural (brick, stone, wood, metal, glass). Structural cells stay static as long as they connect to an anchor through other solid cells. When structural cells are destroyed in a tick, the engine runs a connectivity check over the affected area, and any component that no longer reaches an anchor becomes a rigid body and falls. Undermine a cliff and it drops. Knock out the base of a tower and the tower comes down. Decorations (moss, fungus, vines, leaves: static but not structural) within 24 cells of a detached piece go with it, so they do not stay behind as static cells that pin the falling body in mid-air. Structure painted in mid-air, touching no other structure, is checked too, so it falls.

The check is bounded so that one explosion cannot stall a tick. It searches up to 65,536 cells per component, and a component that hits the limit is treated as supported for that tick and checked again on the next. The first version uses connectivity only. A stress model, where a long beam of weak material can snap under its own weight, comes later if a game needs it.

### Damage and fracture

Every solid cell has hit points, stored in its 8-bit material value. Explosions, impacts, heat, and acid reduce them. A cell at zero becomes the debris its material defines: brick turns to rubble, wood to splinters, glass to shards, and metal to scrap. Depending on the material, debris becomes powder or free particles.

When a body is destroyed in pieces, it re-runs the flood fill and splits into one body per connected part. When one physics step changes a body's velocity, apart from gravity, by more than its fracture strength divided by 32 (in m/s), the body shatters along a Voronoi pattern whose seed points come from the deterministic hash, so the same hit cracks the same way on every machine. Three seed points in four crowd toward the side that took the impact, so a body breaks into small pieces where it hit and large slabs further away. Each piece moves with the parent's velocity at its own center of mass plus a kick of at most 0.25 m/s, so a wreck settles instead of jumping back up. An impact raises dust in proportion to the energy lost, cells times the change in speed squared over 60, up to 10,000 cells: a crate set down raises none, a tower hitting the ground a rolling cloud. Half of a shatter's dust comes out of the cracks and half from the face that hit; a hard landing that breaks nothing still raises its dust. Glass breaks at a 1.9 m/s jolt, wood at 8.1, brick at 10 (about a 5 m drop) and concrete at 16. The test looks at impacts only, so the weight of a resting pile never shatters the bodies at its bottom. A body's fracture strength is the mass-weighted mean of its brittle cells, so one glass pane does not make a brick tower shatter like glass. Steel bends nothing and only loses cells.

A body split into parts builds each part from the parent's own local grid and keeps the parent's rotation, so no cells are lost to rasterizing a rotated part, and each part moves with the parent's velocity at its own center of mass. A body that loses cells but stays in one piece keeps its Rapier body, velocity and contacts and only gets a new collider and mass. Explosions, including those set off by scene events and player tools, push a body at the cell of it nearest the blast, with an impulse that scales with the square root of its mass, so a charge at the foot of a tall tower tips it over. All blasts in one tick together change a body's speed by at most 14 m/s, so a pocket of gunpowder going off at once throws a hut rather than launching it out of the world.

### Bodies in liquids

Bodies and liquids push on each other. When a body is stamped into the grid, liquid and powder cells in its way are moved to the nearest free cell within 4 cells, sideways first. Liquid that finds none goes into the cells the body left at its last unstamp that lie in the liquid (air with liquid beside or over it), lowest first, so a body moving through water takes the water in front of it to the space behind it and leaves no slit or pit; and failing that onto the lowest part of the surface of the pool it lies in, found best-first by height through that liquid (oil through its own slick), so it never piles up over a sinking hull or against a mast. Powder goes up its own column to the top of the powder and takes the place of the liquid there, which then moves as displaced liquid, so a hull settling into a sandy bed raises a mound beside it rather than a tower of sand through the water. Stamping water up a body's outline or up its column to the first air, as the engine once did, stood water 40 to 100 rows over the sea on decks, spars and masts. A body cell whose displaced cell still finds no room is left out of the stamp for that tick, so no liquid or powder is destroyed, and it still counts as wet for buoyancy. Each body has an envelope computed with its shape: the air between its solid cells along each local row and column, which is the inside of a hull, and the air it closes in completely, which is a sealed hold. Air between solid cells of a row with none of the body over it holds water only within 8 cells of a floor below it, the inside of an open boat or the water on a deck, so the air between two masts is no hold. Each tick the engine reads the level of the liquid or powder just outside the body on its left and on its right, reading each column from the bottom up so that drops running down the hull or a splash beside it do not count, and takes the mean of the two, because under a wide hull the cell world carries no pressure from one side to the other. That pressure is stood in for twice each tick, for a body with the medium on both sides. While the liquid runs on under most of its columns, up to 128 cells, two for each row the levels differ, move from the top of the liquid on the high side, highest first, to the air over it on the low side, lowest first, never raising the low side over the high one. And open pits of air in its outline that it does not hold, below the higher level with none of the body over them up to the surface, such as the space between two masts over a sunken deck, fill from the top of the liquid beside it, up to 128 cells a tick: a mast is a thin pole the sea runs past, but to the flow phase it is a wall. Liquids with a lifetime, such as burning oil, are never carried this way. A body lying on the bed is a dam, and nothing moves past it. Every solid cell and every envelope cell below that level displaces the medium, the cell at the level in proportion to how far it is under, and is lifted by the medium's weight at its own position. Liquid or powder that lies inside the envelope, such as water in a flooded hold, weighs on the hull where it lies and displaces nothing. Lift and load act at their own centers rather than at the center of mass, so a hull that tilts finds the lift moving to its lower side and rights itself, and a hull that floods on one side lists that way. A wooden hull 12 m long with a dry hold and iron ballast floats with its deck clear, rights itself from 17 degrees to under 2 within ten seconds, lists when one compartment is holed, and sinks when its hold floods. A sleeping body is woken when its lift or the turning moment of its lift has changed by more than a fiftieth of its weight since it fell asleep. Drag comes from the medium's viscosity in proportion to how much of the body is under. A floating body is never merged into static terrain, and neither is one that rests on another body. Liquid in the air falls past a body and is read as open when the level beside it is measured, and liquid a body pushes aside or lets into its hold lands at rest, its speed taken up by the body.

The cell world carries no pressure, so on its own the sea would neither rise through a hole in a hull's bottom nor above the top of a hole in its side, and a holed hull would float on its hold for ever. The float model stands in for the pressure. An opening is a cell of the hold below the level outside with the liquid outside beside it. The air the openings reach through the hold below that level fills with the liquid, lowest cells first, and while the water inside stands more than a row above the level it runs back out. Each tick it goes whichever way has more cells to move, so the short-lived bubbles moving water leaves under its surface, or a slosh over the level in a flooding hold, do not turn the flow around. Each opening cell passes sqrt(2 g h) cells a tick, water running through an orifice, where h is how far the level outside lies above the opening or above the water inside. The water comes from, and returns to, the surface of the sea that reaches the openings, found by searching up through that water, the highest cells first, so none is made or lost and none moves between separate pools. A 120 by 24 cell hull with an iron floor, holed 7 cells wide through its bottom 8 cells under the sea, takes on 150 cells of water in two seconds and is on the sea floor within 25 seconds; holed low in its side, it floods past the top of the hole and sinks too; with bulkheads, only the holed compartment floods and the hull lists about 10 degrees and floats. A hold floods this way only if it holds at least 64 cells of air, so a crack in a plank is no hold, only if some of that air is open to the outside, so a sealed hold never floods, and only while the medium touches the body on both sides, so a spill running down a cask against a wall is no sea.

When a hull moves, liquid in its hold moves with it: the stamp tells the hold from the sea by where the body was last tick, puts water it displaces inside the hold back into the hold's air, and only pushes sea water aside. So a floating ship that rocks does not pump the sea into its hold. Water one body pushes aside never goes into the air another body holds either, so a stone that heels a ship and sits in the sea on its deck does not push the sea through the deck into the hold under it. The renderer draws the air a body closes in, the air under its deck, a run of a hold's row that is mostly that, such as the hold over a hole in its floor, and air with the body over it under deep water, such as a pocket left in a sunken hull, as the body's dark inside rather than as sky. Air right under liquid with no body between, such as under the spray a blast throws over a ship, is the sky behind the spray.

### Joints

Authored bodies can be connected with Rapier joints: hinges for doors, fixed joints for multi-part structures, and chains of small bodies for ropes and bridges. Each joint has a break force, so a bridge snaps when it is overloaded or its supports are blown off. When a jointed body loses cells but stays in one piece, its center of mass, the origin of its frame, moves, and the joint anchors on it move by the same amount, so a hinge stays on the same material as the body burns.

### Keeping body counts bounded

Destruction produces debris fast, and every body costs physics time. Pieces smaller than 16 cells never become bodies and go straight to particles or powder. A small body that has been at rest for 5 seconds merges back into static terrain. These limits keep a large explosion from producing thousands of tiny bodies.

### Determinism

Rapier uses floats. Cross-platform determinism holds only if Rapier's guarantees hold on every target, and this is the largest technical risk in the project (see Risks). Everything around Rapier, including the connectivity check, the fracture pattern, the displacement search, and the order in which bodies are created and removed, uses integer math and fixed ordering.

## Rendering

The renderer works on the CPU at cell resolution and scales up to pixels, so frames can be rendered headless on any machine, including CI runners without a GPU. The windowed client copies each frame into the window's surface texture through `wgpu`. Cell color comes from the material palette plus a per-cell variant so that sand reads as grains instead of a flat color.

Lighting is where the demos get their look. Emissive materials and hot cells (which glow with their temperature) seed a light field that spreads through open space by repeated sweeps and is then softened with a blur. Sky light falls down each column until it meets a structure, which gives light shafts through cracks; foliage and smoke only dim it. Air underground shows a dim rock back wall that catches nearby light, and rock far from open space sinks into ambient occlusion. Air under an overhang, a bridge or a leaning slab is still sky if open air connects it to the sky within 64 cells (a distance field swept from the sky-lit air), fading to the back wall over the second half of that distance; only enclosed rooms and caves show the back wall. Gas is drawn as a soft volume: its density and color are blurred over about six cells, so smoke, steam, mist and dust read as clouds rather than specks; gas under the open sky is lit by the whole sky dome, and smoke and dust thin out as their lifetime runs down. Deep rock is never flat black: it shows broad low-frequency relief and faint strata, lit by the ambient light and by light blurred in from the lit spaces around it, and rigid bodies are never darkened as far as rock. A rigid body is an object in front of the background, so the sky goes on under a ship's yard or a bridge deck, while the air a body closes in shows its dark inside and the sky does not spread into it. Hot cells glow by temperature, from dull red at 500 C through orange around 1300 C to yellow and white, with a brightness that keeps rising up to about 2000 C, so a white-hot pour and metal on its freezing plateau look different, and lava dims toward red as it nears its freezing point. Wood and leaves do not glow from heat alone; only burning cells do, and their light flickers. Flying particles glow by their own temperature in the same way, so a spark fades from white-gold through orange to dull red as it cools in flight, and one that burns down, such as an ember, fades as it burns out. A fast spark leaves a short fading streak back along its path. The light field keeps only the brightest source at each cell, so a hundred sparks would light the ground no more than one; sparks and blast flashes therefore also add their light to a coarse grid that is summed and spread with a gentle falloff, so a burst of fireworks lights the roofs below it and a large charge lights the whole room for a moment. Bloom, heat haze over hot air, and smoke that scatters light go on top. None of this feeds back into the simulation, so it does not need to be deterministic.

Liquid in the air is drawn with the path it swept. A jet or a thin falling stream is whole cells spaced as far apart as they move in a tick, or a few ticks once the stream thins below a cell a tick, and a still frame showed it as a dotted line. Each cell of a stream is joined to the liquid of its kind behind it on its path, the arc gravity bent its velocity along, as a long exposure blurs a stream, where that liquid lies within 16 ticks of travel (a quarter of a second), up to 48 cells. The cells of a stream follow one path, so the liquid behind a cell is where its own arc back says; traced back along a straight line over four ticks, a long gap missed it, and a trickle off a ledge that left a cell every few ticks was drawn as drops. Then air between two cells of one liquid, one of them in the air, is drawn as that liquid where the two lie along the way the liquid in the air moves (a row, a column or a diagonal, whichever is nearest): a cell of it, or two where both are in the air. Closed along every row, column and diagonal, a scatter of drops at the foot of a jet was drawn as a cross-hatched lattice. Nothing is drawn behind the last cell of a stream, so a falling mass keeps its shape and a lone drop stays a drop. The drawing is a function of the cells in `fe-sim` (`liquid::drawn`), so a scene can check what is drawn with `drawn MATERIAL in X0 Y0 X1 Y1`; it never feeds back into the simulation.

A 1920x1080 frame (960x540 cells) takes about 0.2 s on four cores. That is enough for recording video and for a playable window at smaller sizes, but not the 60 fps at 1440p the original target asked for; moving the light pass to a GPU compute shader is the obvious next step.

## Multiplayer

### Model

Multiplayer uses server-ordered lockstep. Every client and the server run the full simulation. Only inputs travel over the network: a player's movement, aim, and actions for a tick, usually under 16 bytes.

The server collects inputs, stamps each with the tick it applies to, and broadcasts the confirmed input set for each tick. The server does not wait for slow clients. An input that arrives after its tick's deadline is applied on the next open tick. A client simulates tick T only after it has the confirmed inputs for T.

### Latency

Waiting for confirmed inputs puts each client's world about half a round trip behind the server, plus a small buffer. To hide that, the player's own character is predicted locally and moves the moment a key is pressed. If the confirmed result differs, only the character is rewound and replayed, which is cheap because a character is a few hundred bytes of state. The world is never rolled back. A world change the player causes, such as digging, appears once it is confirmed, about one round trip later. A cosmetic preview can cover the gap.

### Desync detection and repair

Every 30 ticks, each client sends the server its state hash split by subsystem: cells, particles, bodies, characters, and game state (tool masks and zones, status text, and script state). When only the cells differ, the server asks for the client's per-chunk hashes at that tick, finds the chunks that differ, and sends its own copies of them and their neighbors, to be applied when the client reaches the server's current tick. When anything else differs, or three reports in a row mismatch, the server sends a full snapshot instead. A desync is treated as a bug: CI runs a soak at 150 ms round trip and 2% loss and fails if the server records any.

### Joining

A late joiner receives a zstd-compressed snapshot of the loaded chunks, the rigid bodies, the particles, the game script's state, and the input log since the snapshot tick, then catches up by simulating forward faster than real time. Every step of the join survives packet loss: a client asks again every 0.5 s until it is welcomed (the server answers a repeated hello with the same welcome), the server keeps cycling the snapshot parts that have not been acknowledged, and a client that already loaded a snapshot acknowledges it again if the server is still sending it.

### Known limit

Every client simulates the active region around every player. If players spread far apart, each machine simulates several separate regions, and the slowest client sets the ceiling. The first version caps the total active area. A later version lets regions far from a client switch to state streamed from the server.

## Scripting

Game rules and mods are WebAssembly modules that run inside the deterministic tick. A scene names one with `script = PATH` in its `[scene]` block. The runtime is [wasmi](https://github.com/wasmi-labs/wasmi), an interpreter, configured so that a script cannot break the determinism contract:

1. Floats are disabled. A module that contains any float instruction or float type is rejected when it loads, with an error that says so. This replaces the earlier plan of running with NaN canonicalization on: integer-only scripts have no rounding or NaN-bit differences to canonicalize.
2. Every call gets a fixed fuel budget (2 million units for `on_tick`, 50 million for `on_init`). Host functions that scan cells charge fuel too. A script that runs out faults at the same instruction on every machine; it is never called again and the fault is shown in the status line.
3. The script's whole state is its linear memory and its mutable globals. After every call both are saved into the simulation, so the state hash and snapshots cover them, and a late joiner or a resynced client restores the script from the snapshot and continues bit for bit. To make that true, a module must export its memory and every mutable global (Rust mods link with `--export=__stack_pointer`), cannot have a start function, and cannot use instructions that change tables or drop segments.

A script imports host functions from module `fe` (all `i32` or `i64`): it reads cells, counts materials in a rectangle, and reads character positions; it queues the same commands the timeline and the control API use (paint, fill, dig, explode, carve, add and remove players); and it sets game state that lives in the simulation: per-player tool masks, per-player tool zones (a tool aimed outside the zone does nothing), and a status string that clients and the demo recorder draw in their HUD. The scene runner calls `on_init` once after setup and `on_tick` at the start of every tick, and applies the queued commands that tick before the step. Script commands are not recorded in replays, because a replay runs the script again.

Game state only enters the world hash when a game sets some, so scenes without a script keep their stored hashes. The join handshake carries a hash of the script bytes next to the material and scene hashes, and the server rejects a client whose script differs.

Mods are written in any language that compiles to integer-only WebAssembly. The first one, Siege builder, is a `no_std` Rust crate in `mods/siege`; `ci/build_mods.sh` rebuilds it into `scenes/games/siege/siege.wasm`, which is committed so that tests and players do not need a wasm toolchain.

## Agent and developer hooks

The engine is meant to be developed by agents as much as by people. Determinism makes most of this cheap, because any run can be reproduced exactly from a seed and an input log.

The headless server binary exposes a JSON-RPC 2.0 control API over stdio and TCP. An MCP server wraps the same API so that Claude Code and similar tools can call it directly. The API covers loading a scene, stepping N ticks, painting and erasing cells, reading back a region as text or raw data, getting world and per-chunk hashes, reading per-phase performance counters, rendering a screenshot to PNG, and saving or loading a replay.

Scenes are plain text files. A scene has a header (size, seed, material set), a legend that maps characters to materials, an ASCII grid, and an optional expectations block, for example that after 120 ticks the region (10, 40)-(30, 50) holds at least 300 water cells, or that the world hash equals a stored value. An agent writes a test by writing one of these files.

The `fe` command-line tool runs scenes, tests, benchmarks, and replays. Three commands matter most for debugging. `fe replay --until <invariant>` finds the first tick where an invariant breaks. `fe diff <run-a> <run-b>` reports the first tick and chunk where two runs differ, which is the main tool for desync bugs. `fe test --bless` updates stored hashes on purpose and prints what changed.

Invariant checks run in debug builds and in CI: mass is conserved for materials with no active reactions, no cell is processed twice in one tick, every cell owned by a rigid body is accounted for, and dirty rects cover every cell that changed. Logging uses `tracing` with JSON output, so agents can parse it instead of scraping text.

## Code quality rules

These rules are enforced in CI, and a pull request that breaks one does not merge.

1. `cargo fmt --check` passes.
2. `cargo clippy --all-targets -- -D warnings` passes with the `pedantic` group on, plus `unwrap_used`, `expect_used`, `undocumented_unsafe_blocks`, and `missing_docs` for public items. Exceptions are allowed only through `#[expect(...)]` with a reason string.
3. Every crate has `#![forbid(unsafe_code)]` except the scheduler crate, whose unsafe code is tested under Miri.
4. Simulation crates deny `float_arithmetic` and the disallowed types listed in the determinism contract.
5. `cargo deny` passes for licenses, advisories, and duplicate dependencies.
6. Every material rule has a scene test. Property tests (`proptest`) cover conservation and order-independence. The scene loader and the network message decoder are fuzzed with `cargo-fuzz`.
7. The determinism tests (thread-count and cross-platform) pass.
8. Benchmarks run in CI with `criterion`, and a slowdown of more than 5% on any tracked benchmark fails the build unless the commit message explains it.

## Performance targets

The reference machine is a desktop with 8 physical cores from 2023 or later and a mid-range discrete GPU. The tick rate is fixed at 60 Hz, so a tick has 16.6 ms in total, of which the cell simulation gets 8 ms.

The targets were set before any code existed, from rough published numbers: the Rust engine [sandengine](https://github.com/Ijtihed/sandengine) reports about 5.6 ns per cell on a saturated grid. The measured column comes from the 4-core cloud container this engine was built in, not the reference machine, so multi-core numbers there understate an 8-core desktop. Several targets are missed, and the table says so rather than moving the targets.

The worst case for the cell passes is fast-falling sand: a cell falling at up to 16 cells per tick does up to 16 steps of work each tick, so "cost per visited cell" includes that. Profiling shows the cost spread across many small steps (about 450 instructions per visited cell) with no single hot spot left after the fixes in phase 2: buffering dirty marks per job instead of using atomics per cell took it from 253 ns to 25 ns.

## Demos

Every demo is a deterministic scene file with a scripted camera and scripted events. The same file renders to video headless, runs as a benchmark, and is checked as a regression test by its world hash. A demo that drifts is a bug.

### Showcase scenes

The showcase scenes are built to look good in a trailer and to push a different part of the engine each.

Foundry is a white-hot metal pour into stone molds. The metal cools through yellow and orange to a dark solid, and where it meets a water channel it throws up steam. Nearly all the light in the scene comes from the metal itself, so it tests emissive lighting, heat transfer, and phase changes together.

Glowcave is a huge cavern under a rainstorm. Water seeps through soil, gathers into waterfalls, and fills pools, while mist drifts in shafts of light from cracks in the ceiling and blue fungus glows along the walls. It is the calm scene, and it tests liquids at scale and soft lighting.

Demolition is a tall tower of brick, timber, and glass. Charges go off floor by floor, the structure breaks into hundreds of rigid bodies that tumble and shatter, dust rolls outward, and the timber catches fire. It tests rigid body creation, splitting, and free particles under load.

Firestorm is a forest on a hillside that catches fire in a crosswind. Leaves flash first, trunks burn slowly, smoke rises and lights orange from below, and the river stops the fire: flames and embers that reach the water go out. It tests fire spread, gases, and wind across a wide active area.

Dam break is the stress test: a reservoir of 4 million water cells released at once into a valley. It doubles as the main benchmark for the 4-million-cell target.

### Multiplayer demos

Each multiplayer demo shows an on-screen overlay with the tick, the world hash, bandwidth per player, round-trip time, and a desync counter.

Shared sandbox puts 8 players in one world, painting, digging, and blowing things up. Its job is the bandwidth graph, which should stay flat while the screen fills with chaos.

Siege is 2v2. Each team builds a fort from a material budget, then the teams bombard each other until the forts collapse into rigid bodies. It shows that rigid body physics stays in sync across machines.

Flood rescue is co-op. Water rises through a cave system and players dig channels to steer it away from villagers. Large active liquid regions make it the stress test for lockstep.

Chaos soak runs bot players under 150 ms of simulated latency and 2% packet loss, with a player joining mid-explosion. The desync counter has to stay at zero for the whole run. It is a test that also looks good on screen.

## Game ideas

The engine is meant to support games well outside Noita's genre. These ideas show the range:

- Siege builder: teams build forts from real materials, then bombard each other. Wood burns, stone cracks, sand fills the breaches. Lockstep keeps a 1v1 with huge destruction cheap on bandwidth.
- Wildfire crew: co-op firefighting where fire spreads by wind, fuel, and moisture. Players dig firebreaks, drop water, and backburn.
- Flooded hull: a co-op submarine where hull breaches flood cell by cell. The crew welds, pumps, and reroutes water through compartments.
- Alchemist's run: a roguelike where the reaction table is shuffled for each seed, so players work out the chemistry by experiment on every run.
- Dig race: competitive mining where you can flood or collapse your opponent's tunnels.
- Rising lava: a last-player-standing game in fully destructible terrain while lava climbs from below.
- Terrarium: a slow god game with rain, erosion, and plants and fungi that grow by cell rules.
- Verified puzzles: physics puzzles where a solution is an input log. The server replays it to check it, so leaderboards cannot be faked.
- Agent arena: AI agents play through the control API against each other or against people, with humans watching.
- Orbital salvage: wrecked ships built from cells that you cut apart with lasers. The pieces drift as rigid bodies in zero gravity.

## Build order

Each phase ends with a demo that has to run before the next phase starts.

1. Phase 0, foundation. Workspace, CI with every quality gate, the scene format, and the `fe` tool skeleton. Exit: an empty scene loads, steps, and hashes in CI on all three platforms.
2. Phase 1, deterministic core. Single-threaded simulation of powder, liquid, gas, fire, temperature, and reactions; the control API and MCP server; a plain windowed viewer with no lighting. Exit: scene tests for every material, the cross-platform hash test, and an agent driving the engine end to end through MCP.
3. Phase 2, parallel and large worlds. The checkerboard scheduler, dirty rects, sleeping chunks, chunk streaming, world generation, and benchmarks. Exit: the thread-count test passes, the per-cell targets are measured and the table above is updated, and Dam break runs at target.
4. Phase 3, rendering. Texture streaming, emissive lighting, bloom, heat haze, smoke scattering, and headless video capture. Exit: Glowcave and Firestorm render to video.
5. Phase 4, bodies and particles. Free particles, pixel rigid bodies, structural support, damage and fracture, buoyancy, joints, and the cross-platform Rapier test. Exit: Demolition and Foundry run, and their hashes match on all platforms.
6. Phase 5, multiplayer. Lockstep protocol, character prediction, desync detection and repair, late join. Exit: all four multiplayer demos, with Chaos soak at zero desyncs.
7. Phase 6, scripting. Game logic and mods in WebAssembly, run by wasmi with floats disabled and a fuel limit per call (see Scripting), plus mod hashes in the join handshake. Exit: a playable prototype of one game idea. The prototype is Siege builder in `scenes/games/siege`: a build phase with a per-team cell budget, then a battle phase that ends when a team's crystal core is gone.

## Risks

Cross-platform float determinism in Rapier is the largest risk. The Rapier docs promise it under conditions, but this project needs it to hold for 1,000,000-tick soaks with hundreds of bodies. Phase 4 tests it first. If it fails, the fallback is a fixed-point rigid body solver written for this engine, which is several weeks of work and would delay multiplayer.

The cost of simulating every player's region on every client sets the real player limit, and it is unknown until Phase 5. Eight players spread across a large world may be too much for the reference machine without the region-streaming fallback.

Desync repair is harder than resending a chunk. Rigid bodies and particles cross chunk borders, so a repair has to move them consistently too. The repair path gets its own fuzz tests.

Lockstep adds input delay to world interaction. Character prediction hides it for movement, but digging and building still lag by one round trip. Whether 150 ms feels acceptable is a playtest question, not an engineering one.

The 32-cell reach limit per tick is set by the checkerboard pattern. Anything faster has to go through the particle system, and materials that need long-range effects in one tick, such as lightning or lasers, need a separate deterministic pass that runs after the four checkerboard passes.

Rendering ended up on the CPU instead of the GPU, so headless screenshots and videos need no GPU at all. They are still for people and agents to look at; world hashes remain the only pass/fail check.

### Risks that came true

The cell pass costs about 25 ns per visited cell on one thread, against a 5 ns target. Most of the remaining cost is the neighbor lookup across chunk borders and the per-cell rule dispatch. A packed 2x2 cell layout or SIMD scanning of air runs are the next things to try.

The CPU renderer draws a 1080p frame in about 0.2 s, roughly 5 frames per second. That is fine for videos and agents but far from 60 fps at 1440p. Moving light propagation and bloom to a compute shader is the fix, and the renderer's output format already matches a texture upload.

Liquid surfaces with a partly filled top row used to never fully sleep, because single cells kept walking along the surface. Bodies at rest are no longer stepped: settle moves only lower cells, a pool the pressure pass has checked carries a settled mark, and a level lake sleeps completely.

Momentum liquids, the rule before the flow phase, moved every liquid cell on its own with a velocity. Their collapses turned into spray, chunk borders shaped the flow into terraces 10 rows high at x 64, 128 and 192, a surge had no run-up or slosh, and the dam break never slept. Moving resting liquid as columns removed all of these: the flow phase has no chunk borders, a body cannot tear, and the column speeds carry the momentum. Two order effects of the passes still showed after that and are fixed. Liquid falling across a chunk border found the liquid below it not yet moved, which the bottom-up fall sweep and the shared rounding fixed. A falling slab's column at the first column of a chunk ran ahead of the slab as a thin vertical stripe, because the neighbor in the later chunk still looked like liquid held up beside a hole and gave it the Torricelli speed; only a floor that cannot move this tick counts for that now.

The shallow water equations describe columns, so liquid cannot overhang. A breaking wave is a sloping front, not a curl, and a jet or a sheet pouring off a brink is cells in the air until it lands. Fast flow over a step in the bed that is deep under water used to put a step in the surface above it, because the solver crossed the step at the deep column's speed rather than with its discharge, and the roller held the flow back over the step; a surge over a 150-row cliff 100 rows under the surface stood up to 25 rows high over the cliff's edge for 200 ticks. Both are fixed (see the flow phase), and the surface now dips over the cliff by the four to six rows that speeding the flow up takes. A slab of liquid falling onto a body lands first where the body is highest, so until the rest of it lands the part that has landed stands as a block with steep sides. Vessels joined below their surfaces level through pipes when a row of columns joins them, and otherwise only through the pressure pass, which searches 4,096 cells and moves cells instead of letting them flow. Liquid in a pipe is one slug with one speed: a pipe that branches is followed one way only, and a hole in a wall that also has an opening at the surface is left to the jet rule. The foundry demo's crucible taps through a 3-row hole in a 10-cell wall, which passes about 2 cells of molten metal per tick (its viscosity of 90 slows it; water would pass 2.3), not the 4.4 the demo was tuned for under the old one-cell rule, so its molds and quench tank still fill later than its expectations assume, and five of its twelve checks fail until the demo is retuned. A sheet now leaves a brink at a cell per tick or faster, so the surge when the crucible is broken open at tick 900 clears the first mold, which the slower sheet used to fill (31 cells of metal in it at tick 1000 against 529).

Falling liquid no longer reads as bars or drops in the review scenes. In the dam break over a stone block, single columns stood 10 to 14 rows above the flood past the block on 11 of the ticks from 300 to 500 sampled every four; now the tallest is 7 rows, once. Jets through walls 4 to 6 cells thick, a pool's drain through a hole a cell wide, a tap's drips and the trickle off a ledge are drawn as one line to the end. What is left in the dam break is the hydraulic jump in front of the block: from tick 160 to 290 the fast, shallow flood runs into the block and stands up against its upstream face as a jump one column wide and 20 to 28 rows high (at tick 250, 26 rows of water at x 558 and 48 at x 559). The shallow water equations make a jump a step one column wide, and the roller only slopes its front; a real jump that high would spread over a hundred columns. Making the face tumble back down itself narrowed the step to 11 to 14 rows but broke the U-tube's swing, so it is not done. Past the block, from tick 420 to 480, the lee by the lip dips into V-shaped notches 10 to 14 rows deep on 4 of 31 ticks sampled every two (in the rejected build, up to 18 rows deep on 15), and a few cells of air (1 to 7) show between columns at x 600 to 606 on 11 of 16 ticks sampled every four (in the rejected build, up to 13 cells on 13). The sheet off the block traps pockets of air of up to about 70 cells where it plunges, and in the grid, though not as drawn, the thick sheet is a comb of columns a cell apart. A floating crate bobs about 5 rows every 60 ticks or so and never settles.

A large deep body takes long to settle. The demo dam break, a 4,080-column lake about 640 rows deep after the break, sloshes for most of a minute and a half and sleeps by tick 5,700, about 95 seconds of game time. Real water that size would slosh for longer.

Every material used to have the same heat capacity, so air cooled hot metal as fast as stone did, and air conductivity was set very low to work around it. Materials now have a heat capacity and transitions a latent heat (see the world model), so air conducts normally and holds little heat, and a pour stays liquid while it fills its molds. Cooling into the surroundings is still conduction only; there is no separate radiation or Newtonian cooling term.

Buoyancy used to be applied at the center of mass with no righting torque, and only the cells below the first one in each column that touched water counted, so a wide hull with a dry hold could not float with its deck clear and demos held ships up with pins. Lift now comes from the body's envelope and acts where it is centered (see bodies in liquids). The cell world still carries no pressure. Holds stand in for it by flooding to the level of the sea outside (see bodies in liquids), but the sea itself does not level under a hull or an ice floe, so the sea on the two sides of a sinking hull can stand at different levels for a while; the hull reads the mean of the two, and a flooding hold draws its water from the side its openings are on, which can then stand lower than the other.
